Working hypotheses — not diagnoses

1. Temperature / seasonality: Cooler conditions could reduce feeding, metabolism and egg production. The temporal pattern is compatible with a contribution, but October’s early low rate and subsequent rebound prevent a simple temperature-only explanation. New daily enclosure measurements would be needed to test the relationship.
2. Food freshness / nutritional intake: Wilting leaves may reduce feeding quality or intake. Previous egg-size recovery after fresh guava/wax apple in August offers a historical analogy, not causal proof. The newly arrived 5 November leaves provide an opportunity to observe any subsequent recovery.
3. Reproductive senescence: At 145 and 122 adult days, Bethany and Liza are no longer newly mature. An age-associated reduction in laying is plausible, but no species-specific lifetime production curve, individual egg series or physiological evidence in this record establishes senescence.
4. Normal variability / data capture: Oviposition, leaf intake, delayed collections, eggs hidden in enclosure structures, and unreported inspection days can all alter apparent short-term collection frequency. The three eggs recovered 4 November demonstrate that routine inspection can miss previously laid eggs.

Current assessment: The causes may coexist. The early-November decline is a signal to monitor, not by itself evidence of illness or egg retention. No temperature manipulation or other husbandry intervention is justified solely to achieve a numerical laying target while the insects remain otherwise well.

Proposed non-disruptive follow-up (5–19 November)

- Record routine eggs daily or at each visit; keep historical recoveries separately classified.
- Record when the new leaves are actually installed, their species/freshness and when they next wilt; note any visible feeding change.
- Retain daily enclosure temperature and relative-humidity summaries where available, rather than extrapolating from outdoor weather.
- Seek individual laying attribution opportunistically, without disturbing the females just to obtain a count.
- Note abnormal egg size, shape, pigmentation or fragility and any changes in feeding, grip, mobility, abdomen or general activity.
- Compare subsequent 7–14 days with the preceding collection pattern, while acknowledging accumulation and collection-date uncertainty. A rebound following new foliage would support an association, not prove that food quality alone caused the reduction.
